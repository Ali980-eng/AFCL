# AFCL Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

> **Important Notice**
>
> This document is provided for explanatory purposes only. It does not override, modify, or replace the AFCL framework or any underlying software license. In case of conflict, the official AFCL framework definition prevails.

---

## 1. What is AFCL?

AFCL (Author Fair Conditions License) is a **licensing framework** that defines rules for how developers or authors may write additional licensing conditions.

It does not function as a standalone software license. Instead, it acts as a **meta-layer** that governs the structure, clarity, and interpretation of license conditions attached to any base license.

---

## 2. Is AFCL a software license?

No.

AFCL is not a software license and does not grant or restrict software usage rights directly.

Software rights are defined by the **underlying license** chosen by the developer (e.g., MIT, GPL, LGPL, proprietary licenses).

AFCL only governs the **format and validity of additional conditions** written by the developer.

---

## 3. Does AFCL replace MIT, GPL, or LGPL?

No.

AFCL does not replace or override existing licenses.

Developers may freely use any base license, and AFCL operates as a **structural framework above it**, ensuring that any added conditions remain clear, measurable, and consistent.

---

## 4. What does AFCL actually govern?

AFCL governs:

* How developer-defined conditions must be written
* How ambiguity is interpreted
* Whether conditions are valid or invalid based on clarity rules
* Whether conditions are measurable or objectively interpretable

It does NOT govern:

* Copyright ownership
* Source code rights
* Distribution rights
* Licensing permissions of the base software license

---

## 5. Can developers define their own conditions?

Yes.

Developers may define custom conditions, restrictions, or permissions.

However, these conditions must comply with AFCL requirements:

* They must be clear and unambiguous
* They must be objectively interpretable
* They should be measurable when possible
* They must not contradict AFCL framework rules

---

## 6. What happens if a condition is ambiguous?

If a developer-defined condition can reasonably be interpreted in multiple ways, the interpretation that is:

> **least restrictive to the user**

will apply.

If a condition is fundamentally unclear or unmeasurable, it may be considered invalid under AFCL rules.

---

## 7. Can AFCL be used with proprietary software?

Yes.

AFCL is independent of whether a project is:

* Open source
* Source available
* Closed source
* Commercial software

It can be applied to any licensing model as a structural framework for defining conditions.

---

## 8. Does AFCL require software to be open source?

No.

AFCL does not impose open-source requirements.

The openness or closed nature of software is determined exclusively by the underlying license chosen by the developer.

---

## 9. Does AFCL itself grant permissions to users?

No.

AFCL does not grant usage rights, copying rights, modification rights, or distribution rights.

All such permissions come from the base license (e.g., MIT, GPL, LGPL).

---

## 10. Why is AFCL licensed under the MIT License?

AFCL is licensed under the MIT License to ensure:

* Free use of the framework
* Ability to integrate it into any licensing model
* Ability to modify and extend the framework
* Maximum compatibility with existing ecosystems

---

## 11. Who enforces AFCL rules?

Enforcement depends on the legal system governing the base license and the project.

AFCL provides a **formal interpretation framework**, but actual enforcement depends on the jurisdiction and the underlying license agreement.

---

## 12. What is the main goal of AFCL?

The main goal of AFCL is to:

* Improve clarity in developer-defined license conditions
* Reduce ambiguity in interpretation
* Provide consistent evaluation rules for custom conditions
* Support transparent and structured licensing design

---

## 13. Can AFCL conditions conflict with the base license?

No.

AFCL conditions cannot override or contradict the underlying software license.

The base license always defines the actual legal permissions for the software.
